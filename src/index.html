      How this was made

      Each dot is one object from GCAT, the catalog of everything launched into space kept by astronomer Jonathan McDowell. A dot appears on the day its object reached orbit, or broke away from another object, and disappears on the day it reentered or was destroyed. The counts come straight from those dates.

      Each orbit has its real height, shape and tilt from the catalog: today's orbit for objects still up, and the orbit the catalog records for the rest. That is why the rings and shells look the way they do: the crowd in low orbit, the Starlink shells below 500 km, and the ring of geostationary satellites 35,786 km above the equator.

      The catalog doesn't say which way each orbit faces or where along it each object is, so those are set at random, the same way on every visit. Objects from one launch share an orbit, satellites from one launch leave in a line and spread out over the following weeks, and the pieces of a breakup start where the broken object was. So the dots' exact positions are illustrative, while their heights, tilts and numbers are real.

      Debris clouds spread out the way real ones do: pieces thrown onto slightly larger orbits fall behind, smaller ones get ahead, and Earth's equatorial bulge slowly turns each orbit. Motion along the orbits is sped up between 40 and 600 times: slowest while a ship is on screen, 110 times in the slow-motion moments.

      Every launch since 1957 is a brief orange pulse at its launch site, red when it failed to reach orbit; the bars along the timeline count the launches that made it each year. They come from GCAT's launch log.

      The Country switch in the legend recolors everything by who owns it: NASA, SpaceX, the rest of the US (military, other agencies, companies and universities), Russia and the Soviet Union, China, India, Japan, Europe (ESA, EUMETSAT and the European countries) and everyone else. It uses GCAT's owner and state codes; debris and rocket stages count for the owner of the object they came from. Launches count for the agency that launched them.

      The search box finds any object in the catalog by name, catalog number or international ID, and any launch by its payloads, rocket or flight. A result opens its card: for an object, its orbit drawn to scale with Earth and a ring on it on the globe, with the date moved to when it reached orbit; for a launch, the rocket, the site and what it carried.

      The ships are shown at true proportions, with their real heights and a scale bar in metres; the lineup at the end puts the rockets side by side at one scale. Dates and times are the launch, in UTC, and the marker on the globe is the launch site. While the timeline plays it stops at each milestone long enough to read its card; click any mark on the timeline to open one.

      What's left out

      Objects that left for deep space, spacecraft while docked to a station or carried by the Space Shuttle, and anything with no recorded orbit. The catalog is current to %UPDATED%.
